Joint Density Lab

Two random variables, X and Y, and the surface that describes them together, the joint density pX,Y(x, y). Cut the surface at a value of x and measure the area of the cut: that is the marginal pX(x), one slice at a time. Change how the two variables are tied together and watch the marginals stay exactly the same while the joint changes shape. Then colour the plane by the sign of (x − μX)(y − μY) and see what the covariance is measuring.

What to notice

  • The marginal is the area of the slice. Press "Sweep the slice": x0 runs across the plane, the slice pX,Y(x0, y) is drawn on the right as a curve in y, and its area is plotted as a dot above the plane. The dots trace the top curve, pX(x) = ∫ pX,Y(x, y) dy: the marginal of X is the joint with Y integrated out, one value of x at a time. The same for pY, slicing at a value of y. The area of a slice never exceeds 1/(√2π σX), the peak of the marginal.
  • The marginals do not determine the joint. Move ρ from −0.95 to 0.95: the heatmap turns from a cloud tilted one way to a cloud tilted the other, the two marginal curves on the sides do not move at all. The bottom strip makes the point with three joints side by side: the independent one, the product pX(x) pY(y); the Gaussian with the chosen ρ; and two crossed Gaussians. Same μX, σX, μY, σY in all three, to the last digit of the table. Knowing how X is distributed and how Y is distributed says nothing about how they move together: that information lives only in the joint.
  • Covariance is a question of quadrants. The plane is cut at the means. In the quadrants above–above and below–below the product (x − μX)(y − μY) is positive, blue; in the other two it is negative, red. With ρ = 0.7 the probability of each blue quadrant is 0.373 and of each red one 0.127, the mass concentrates along the diagonal, and the covariance is the blue mass minus the red mass, each point weighted by how far it is from the means: 0.70 for unit standard deviations. Set ρ = 0 and the four quadrants hold 0.25 each. Tick the box to see the integrand itself: it vanishes along the two lines through the means, which is why points near the means, however many, contribute nothing.
  • Uncorrelated is not independent. Choose "two crossed Gaussians": the mass concentrates along both diagonals, the blue quadrants and the red ones hold 0.25 each, the covariance is zero to the precision of the grid. Yet the joint is nothing like the product of the marginals in the first panel of the strip, and the slice at x0 = 2 shows two bumps, at y ≈ +1.8 and y ≈ −1.8: knowing X tells a lot about Y. Independence implies zero covariance; the converse is false, and this is the counterexample.
  • The draws agree with the surface. The dots are samples from the joint; their sample covariance, printed under the quadrant plot, approaches the integral as the number of draws grows, with the usual fluctuations of a few hundredths at 3000 draws. This is the only place in the page where randomness enters: every curve and every number in the table comes from the density itself.

The problem being solved

The joint density of the lesson, for two scalar random variables. The Gaussian joint with means μX, μY, standard deviations σX, σY and correlation ρ is

p(x,y) = 1 / (2π σX σY √(1−ρ²)) · exp( −[ u² − 2ρ u v + v² ] / (2(1−ρ²)) ),   u = (x−μX)/σX, v = (y−μY)/σY

and its marginals are the Gaussians N(μX, σX) and N(μY, σY) whatever ρ is; ρ = 0 gives the product of the marginals, that is, independence. The "two crossed Gaussians" are the mixture ½ p+0.9(x,y) + ½ p−0.9(x,y) of two such densities with opposite correlation: each has the same marginals, so the mixture has them too, and its covariance is the average of +0.9 σXσY and −0.9 σXσY, zero.

The page evaluates p(x,y) on a 140 × 140 grid covering ±4.2 standard deviations around the means, and computes everything else by sums on the grid: the marginals as the sums of the rows and of the columns times the step, the means and variances from the marginals, the covariance and the quadrant probabilities from the joint,

pX(x) = ∫ p(x,y) dy,   μX = ∫ x pX(x) dx,   σ²X = ∫ (x−μX)² pX(x) dx,   cov = ∬ (x−μX)(y−μY) p(x,y) dx dy

so that what is printed is what the definitions say, up to the discretization, which is why the table shows the input values back with three decimals. The draws use the Box–Muller transform for independent standard normals and the Cholesky factor of the covariance matrix to correlate them.
