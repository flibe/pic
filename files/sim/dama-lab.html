Dama Lab

An agent learns Italian draughts from nothing but the result of complete games, with the Monte Carlo control of the lesson: a table of action values, one entry per board it has produced, an ε-greedy policy, and after every game the sample return of the game averaged into the entries of the moves it played. Start on a small board, where a table is enough, watch it learn, and then switch to the real 8×8 board and watch the table explode.

What to notice

  • Learning is visible, and slow. Against the opponent that counts pieces one move ahead, on the 6×6 board with three pieces each, random play wins about 3 games in 100. The agent wins about a quarter of its first 2000 games, half of the games around the 10 000th, and about two in three after 50 000, still improving. One game teaches it little: the return of the game is averaged into the fifteen or so entries of the moves it played, and nothing else moves. Press "10 000 games" a few times and watch the green curve.
  • Nothing is learnt until the game is over. Step through a game: the values printed on the squares do not change while it is played. Monte Carlo does not bootstrap, the target of an update is the complete return of the game, available only at its end. The opening chart shows the consequence: Q of the best first move is the fraction of games won after that move, and it climbs one game at a time, with no propagation from the positions that follow. Compare with the Tic-Tac-Toe Lab, where a backup moves a value towards the value of the next board after every move.
  • Decreasing ε wins. With ε = 0.1 kept constant the agent wins about 67% of the games around the 50 000th; with the box "decrease ε gradually" ticked, about 87%. The reason is the one of the lesson: an ε-soft policy converges to the best ε-soft policy, which throws away one move in ten, and only by reducing ε does the agent get close to the deterministic optimal policy. A larger ε is worse again: with ε = 0.3 it wins half of the games at best. And ε = 0 is not a trap here either, for the same reason as in the tic-tac-toe: every untried move starts at 0.5, so a move whose entry falls below 0.5 is abandoned for an untried one, and the optimistic start does the exploring. The greedy agent ends around 70%, with a wide spread from one run to the next, since a greedy run depends on which lines it happens to try first.
  • The sample average remembers the bad old days. Switch the step to a constant α = 0.1 and the agent does better, about 74% against 67%. With 1/n every game counts the same, including the early ones played with a poor policy; but the policy improves as the table fills, so the returns of a given move are not stationary, and a constant step, which forgets the past, tracks them better. This is the non-stationary bandit of section 2.5 of Sutton and Barto, inside policy iteration.
  • First-visit or every-visit makes little difference on this board, where a position rarely repeats within a game; every-visit gets a few more samples from the games with kings moving back and forth, and here it learns a little faster.
  • The real board is out of reach for a table. Choose the 8×8 board with twelve pieces each: two random players meet more than a million different boards in 20 000 games, and the agent meets a new board at almost every move it makes. After 10 000 games against the counting opponent it has not won a single game, and against the random opponent it wins 55% where random play already wins 47%. The entries of the table are 230 000 and almost all of them hold one single return. This is the wall that the next lessons climb with function approximation: one number per board does not scale, and a value must be computed from features of the board instead. The table stops growing at two million entries.
  • Playing itself. With "itself" as opponent the two colours move with the same table, each reading the board from its own side, and the return of the game is averaged into the entries of both, as 1 and 0. The curve then says nothing about strength, since the opponent improves with the agent and the games stay near even, with black slightly ahead on the small boards. Judge it by sitting down against it instead: "Play against it".

The problem being solved

Italian draughts, simplified: men move one square diagonally forward and capture forward by jumping over an adjacent enemy man onto the empty square beyond, continuing while they can; a man that reaches the last row becomes a king, which moves and captures one square in all four directions; men cannot capture kings; captures are compulsory, and when several are available one that takes the most pieces must be chosen. A player who cannot move loses; forty moves without a capture or a promotion, or three hundred moves in all, are a draw. The finer Italian rules on which capture to prefer are left out.

The episode is one game, the state is the board with the side to move, the actions are the legal moves, the reward is 1 for a win, 0.5 for a draw and 0 for a loss at the end of the game, zero before, and γ = 1. The table holds Q(s, a), stored under the board that the move produces, so two moves that lead to the same board share one entry, and under the board as seen by the player who moves, so the same table serves both colours; the number printed on a square is the Q of the move that ends there, 0.5 until that move has been tried. Before each move the agent is ε-greedy:

with probability 1−ε: the move with the largest Q (ties at random)  ·  with probability ε: a legal move at random

which is the ε-soft policy of the lesson, every move having probability at least ε/|A(s)|. When the game ends with return G, every entry of a move the agent played is updated, once per game in the first-visit version:

N(s,a) ← N(s,a) + 1  ·  Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + (1/N(s,a)) [ G − Q(s,a) ]   (or with a constant α in place of 1/N)

so that Q(s, a) is the sample average of the returns that followed the move: Monte Carlo control with an ε-soft policy, section 5.4 of Sutton and Barto, with policy improvement after every game. The opponent does not learn. "At random" picks a legal move uniformly; "counts pieces one move ahead" picks the move that maximizes its material minus the agent's after the move, minus the most the agent can capture in reply, a king counting 1.6 men, ties at random. The highlighted path is the last move, blue when greedy, amber when exploratory, red for the opponent, with a cross on each captured piece.
