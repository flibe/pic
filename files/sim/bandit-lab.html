Slot Machine Lab

Three slot machines, each paying a number drawn from a distribution the player does not know. The agent of the lesson keeps a sample average of what each machine has paid and pulls, most of the time, the one with the highest estimate; with probability ε it pulls a machine at random. The casino runs in your browser, as fast as you like, and you choose ε, the step size and the initial guess.

What to notice

  • With ε = 0 the agent gets stuck. Set ε to zero and restart a few casinos. All estimates start at zero, the first pull goes to a machine chosen at random, and as soon as that machine pays a positive number its estimate is the largest: the greedy agent never leaves it, whether it is the best one or not. The averaged panel shows the consequence: the share of pulls on the best machine stays near one third, because the first machine is the best one in one case out of three.
  • ε = 0.1 finds the best machine quickly and then wastes a tenth of its pulls. Every exploratory pull goes to a random machine, the best one included, so the share of pulls on the best machine settles at 90% plus a third of 10%, about 93%, and not at 100%.
  • ε = 0.01 is slow and ends up better. It needs many more pulls to try every machine enough times, but once the estimates are right it wastes only 1% of the pulls. In the averaged panel its payout curve starts well below the one of ε = 0.1 and catches up with it towards the end of the thousand pulls; over 2000 pulls it puts 99% of them on the best machine against 94%. Which value of ε is better depends on how long you plan to play.
  • A fixed ε is not the best one can do: decrease it as the estimates settle. A constant ε keeps paying for exploration after it has stopped being useful, which is why ε = 0.1 never passes 93%. Tick "let ε decrease": the agent explores a lot at first, when the estimates are poor, and less and less afterwards, and in the averaged panel the share of pulls on the best machine climbs above 99% while the payout curve ends above both fixed values. The schedule has to go to zero slowly enough that every machine is still tried infinitely often (the sum of the εt must diverge, and 1/t does): this is what guarantees that the estimates still converge. The argument holds because the machines do not change; with payouts that drift over time, the agent would need to keep exploring, and a constant ε is the usual choice.
  • Optimism replaces exploration. Put ε back to zero and raise the initial estimate to 20, above any payout. Every machine disappoints the agent in turn, so every machine is tried, and the greedy agent ends on the best one in about nine runs out of ten, with no random pulls at all; on the machines of the lesson, further apart, in practically all of them. This works because the machines do not change; it would not help if they did.
  • 1/n or a constant step. With the step 1/n the estimate is the sample average and it settles down, because each new number weighs less and less. With a constant α the estimate keeps moving by a fraction α of the last surprise and never settles; what it buys is the ability to follow a machine whose payout drifts, which the casino of this page does not do.
  • The machines of the lesson are the easy case. Switch to them, 3, 1 and 10: the best one stands out after a handful of pulls and every method finds it quickly. Choose instead the machines drawn at random, whose expected payouts can differ by less than the noise of a single pull: even 2000 pulls can leave the estimates in the wrong order.

The problem being solved

The multi-armed bandit of the lesson. There is one state, so the value function depends on the action only: the value of a machine is the expected number it displays,

q*(a) = E{ Rt | At = a }

and the agent has to learn it from the numbers it sees. After the machine has been pulled n−1 times its estimate is the sample average, kept in the incremental form of the lesson,

Qn+1(a) = Qn(a) + (1/n) · ( Rn − Qn(a) )

which is the general shape old value ← old value + step · (target − old value), with the last payout as target. The constant-step option replaces 1/n with α. The policy is ε-greedy:

At = argmaxa Q(a) with probability 1−ε,
a random machine with probability ε

ties in the argmax are broken at random. Each machine pays a normally distributed number with the expected value shown as a dashed line and a spread of its own; the agent sees the numbers, never the distributions. "Total paid" is the return, the sum of the rewards, which is what the agent is trying to maximize.

The page opens on three machines paying 4, 6 and 8 on average, each with a spread of 2: closer than the 3, 1 and 10 of the lesson, which are one click away, and therefore harder to tell apart. The other casinos draw the expected payouts at random. The averaged panel repeats the run 500 times with fresh luck, keeping the machines fixed, and plots the mean at every pull.
