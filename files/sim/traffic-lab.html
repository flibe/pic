Traffic Light Lab

Two one-way roads crossing, the intersection of the lesson. On the left the lights follow a fixed plan, the one that is set once and repeats all day; on the right they are decided at every step by an MPC that looks at the queues it measures and at the sixty seconds ahead. The two intersections receive exactly the same cars at exactly the same times, so whatever difference you see is the controller and nothing else.

What to notice

  • The fixed plan is right only for the traffic it was designed for. With 700 vehicles an hour from the west and 300 from the south, an even split gives the busy road the same green as the quiet one: its queue grows, the other light is green over an empty road. The MPC gives green where the cars are, and the two queues end up of comparable length.
  • Giving the fixed plan the right split moves the problem, it does not solve it. Set the green from the west to 45 s and the one from the south to 20 s: the average wait of the fixed plan improves, from about 94 s to 83 s. But the split is now wrong for the rush at the tenth minute, and the road from the south pays for it — the longest wait of the day goes from 220 s to 455 s, while the MPC, which re-decides every five seconds, stays around 100 s.
  • The minimum yellow is a safety constraint, and it is expensive. Every change of phase costs that yellow, during which nobody moves, so the longer it has to be the less often it is worth switching. Taking it from 5 s to 10, 15 and 25 s, the MPC switches 124, 72, 56 and 36 times over the half hour, and the average wait goes from 26 s to 43, 86 and 132 s. The constraint is not negotiable — it is what makes the intersection safe to cross — and the controller can only arrange the rest around it.
  • A longer prediction window stops paying. The window improves the result until it covers the time the queues need to empty; beyond that the controller is planning for arrivals it only knows on average, and the extra horizon buys nothing.
  • Queues and waiting times are not the same goal. The cost minimizes the queues; the waiting times are measured car by car by the simulator. The difference between the two shows up when one road is much busier than the other: put 1150 vehicles an hour from the west and 100 from the south, with the rush off. With no weight on the road kept waiting, the few cars from the south wait up to 430 s, because a short queue contributes little to the cost. At 0.5 the longest wait falls to 195 s and even the average improves; push the weight to 3 and the worst wait goes down to 165 s while the average climbs to 44 s: the trade-off of the lesson, measured.

The problem being solved

Every 5 s the MPC looks at the queues measured at the two stop lines and chooses the colours for the next 60 s, then applies the first step and throws the rest away. The queues are the state, the colours are the control:

The second line is the safety constraint on the yellow: the timer counts how long the yellow has been displayed, and the light cannot turn red before that timer has reached the minimum. There is no minimum green: a green may end after any step, and what discourages the controller from switching all the time is the yellow itself, during which neither road moves.

The second term of the cost is the one of the lesson on the waiting times, in the simplest form that fits an intersection with two lights: w̄i|k is the longest time either road has been held at red, an upper bound over the two roads, and pushing it down keeps the controller from starving the short queue.|k is an upper bound of the time already spent by the cars still in the queues, and pushing the bound down pushes down the longest wait. The arrivals the controller predicts are the average rate of each road, not the arrivals that actually happen: the forecast is right on average and wrong at every single step, which is the normal condition of a real controller.

The transition constraints, the mutual exclusion and the minimum yellow leave very few admissible colour sequences over a window of twelve steps — a few hundred — so the page finds the best one by exploring them all, with a bound that cuts the branches that are already worse than the best found. The result is the exact optimum of the problem above, which is what the mixed-integer formulation of the lesson would return; writing it as a MILP is what you have to do when the intersection is larger, and the number of sequences explodes.

Simplifications: no vehicles pass on yellow, the saturation flow is the same on both roads, the two roads are one-way and have no turns, and the queues are counted in whole vehicles only in the simulator — inside the controller they are continuous, as in the aggregate model of the lesson.

Where this runs for real

Signal control of this kind is not only a classroom exercise. Three systems are worth looking at, and they differ in exactly the way the lesson distinguishes.

  • Surtrac, Pittsburgh. Every intersection re-plans on a rolling horizon about once a second and sends its neighbours the flow it is about to release: decentralized MPC under another name. The pilot on nine intersections in East Liberty reported travel times, stops and waiting times down by more than a quarter. the field-test paper, the US DOT evaluation.
  • TUC, from the Technical University of Crete. Network-wide signal control written on a store-and-forward model and solved as a quadratic program on a rolling horizon, first tested in the field in Glasgow and then in Chania, Munich and Southampton. the rolling-horizon formulation.
  • UTOPIA, born in Turin at the Fiat Research Centre with Italtel and Mizar, running in the city since the nineteen-eighties and now in tens of European cities: adaptive, and hierarchical and decentralized like the architecture we drew. the catalogue record.
  • SCOOT and SCATS, the two most widely installed adaptive systems in the world, work in a third way: they adjust cycle, split and offset by small increments as the measured flows change, without optimizing a cost over a horizon. They sit between the two columns of this page. SCATS.
