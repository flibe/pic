PEV Aggregator Lab

An aggregator operates the chargers of a fleet of electric vehicles. It has sold the grid a power profile it promises the fleet will follow, and at the same time every driver has asked for a charge by a given hour. The two goals pull against each other, and the controller has to choose. This is the aggregator problem of the lesson, solved in your browser.

What to notice

  • Watch the hour the request arrives. Until then the controller is spreading the charge to follow a flat profile. At that hour the stop appears in the part of the reference that is still ahead, and the plan changes shape at once: the cars that can be moved take their energy earlier, so that the window can be left empty. Nothing about the past changes — only what is still to be decided.
  • Flexibility is parked time, not power. A car that stays eight hours to take three hours of charge can be moved anywhere inside its window; one that arrives nearly empty and leaves in two hours cannot be moved at all. Announce the stop later, or shorten the stays, and the fleet can no longer move its consumption away from the window.
  • Charging on arrival serves neither goal. The dotted line is the fleet without a controller: all the power is drawn when the cars arrive and none afterwards. It follows no profile, and it does not serve the drivers any better — they receive the same charge, only earlier than their deadline required.
  • The priority slider is the weight of the lesson. Towards the grid, the controller sacrifices the drivers' requests to follow the profile; towards the drivers, it serves every request and follows the profile with what is left over. Sweeping it traces the Pareto frontier of the two goals, the picture of the previous lesson.
  • The request can be impossible, and the problem must survive it. Ask for a stop that covers the only hours in which a car is there, and the problem remains solvable: the relaxation variables take the violation, and the table says how much charge was not delivered. A hard deadline would simply report infeasible and leave the controller with nothing to apply.
  • V2G buys the ability to go down. With discharging allowed the fleet can hold the profile even when the request goes below what the cars already need, at the price of energy that has to be bought again later.

The problem being solved

At every hour the controller re-solves the problem over what is left of the day, on the vehicles connected or expected — the set PEVi|k of the lesson — starting from the charge they have now, and applies only the first step. The reference it uses is the one known at that hour: before the announcement, the stop is not in it.

min   d1 Σi (Pi|k − Prefi|k)2  +  d3 Σp εp

Pi|k = Σp (Pchi|k,p − Pdisi|k,p)
xi+1|k,p = xi|k,p + ηch T Pchi|k,p − (1/ηdis) T Pdisi|k,p
xFp|k,p ≥ xrefp − εp    εp ≥ 0    0 ≤ xi|k,p ≤ xmaxp

The deadline of each driver is the relaxed constraint of the lesson: the slack εp may be positive, and it is paid for in the cost with the weight d3, which is what the priority slider moves. A small d3 lets the controller disappoint a driver to follow the grid better; a large one makes it disappoint a driver only when the request cannot be met at all.

Two notes on how this page differs from the blackboard. The square of the deviation is written as a cost in pieces of increasing price — the chords of a parabola — which keeps the problem linear; with the checkbox off the penalty becomes the plain absolute value, and the solution changes shape, because the square spreads the error over many hours while the absolute value concentrates it in few. And the minimum charging power of a real charger, the semi-continuous variable of the lesson, is left out: it would turn this linear program into a mixed-integer one, as in the Building Energy Lab.

The solver is the same simplex written for the building lab, and the model of this page was checked against the Cbc solver on random instances, optimum against optimum.
